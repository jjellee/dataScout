# Navitas Semiconductor Corporation — M&A announcement (2026-10-20)

## 요약(stockanalysis 자동 생성)

### Deal rationale and strategic fit

- Claros acquisition closed, adding integrated voltage regulation close to the processor and completing the grid-to-XPU power portfolio alongside GaN and SiC.
- The combined technologies target lower losses and power-density constraints across the power chain, enabling more usable power for AI compute.
- Claros IVR complements, rather than duplicates, GaN and SiC: it addresses final processor-stage regulation while the existing technologies address conversion and distribution.


### Financial terms and conditions

- The identified 2030 SAM expands from $3.5B to more than $8B, including JFET and Claros IVR; AI infrastructure represents more than $6.9B and nearly 85% of SAM.
- Claros acquisition does not materially change the break-even plan; the stated priorities remain revenue scale, margin expansion and progress toward profitability.
- SAM figures represent addressable market, not revenue guidance.


### Integration plans and timeline

- Following the acquisition close, Claros founder Dan Kultran became VP and GM of the newly created IVR business unit.
- Packaged IVR is intended to enable earlier customer adoption; embedded IVR supports deeper processor integration over time.
- Vertical power delivery moves regulators beneath the processor, while embedded power delivery integrates conversion into the processor package or substrate; embedded delivery is the long-term destination.


### Market and competitive impact

- The strategy pivots from consumer markets toward high-power applications, with AI infrastructure at the center; mobile revenue is expected to be insignificant by end-2026.
- Target markets include AI data centers, grid and energy infrastructure, high-performance computing, and industrial electrification.
- Opportunities span SiC in high-power supplies and solid-state transformers, SiC and GaN in 800V power sidecars, GaN in native 800V compute trays, and IVR near processors.
- Next-generation processor power is projected to rise from ~1.4 kW in 2025 to 4.4 kW in 2028 and well above 10 kW in subsequent years.


### Potential risks and mitigations

- The stated growth task is to convert the expanded portfolio and addressable market into customer adoption and sustainable growth.
- Execution priorities include scaling, reliable U.S.-based manufacturing and supply chain, and a foundry-plus model supported by strategic partnerships.
- Packaged IVR offers a nearer-term adoption route while embedded IVR supports deeper integration as processor architectures evolve.


### Long-term strategic benefits

- The integrated IVR platform scales from 10A building blocks to multi-chip systems; PowerArray coordinates chips and load sharing for differing processor needs.
- The portfolio is designed to improve efficiency, reduce conversion stages and increase power density, helping turn more available power into useful AI compute.

**Llew Vaughan-Edmunds**  
*Chief Marketing Officer / Navitas Semiconductor*

Thanks, Chris. This slide really sets up the problem we are trying to solve. We call it the AI infrastructure power wall. It is a series of compounding challenges from conversion losses and thermal constraints to power density limitations, all the way from the grid to the processor. If we look at today's AI data center power architecture, from left to right, there are challenges developing at every stage. First, we start with traditional line frequency transformers. These have been in existence for over 140 years. They are bulky, heavy, and increasingly incompatible with the power density demanded by modern AI infrastructure. As data center power requirements move into hundreds of megawatts and ultimately gigawatts, the industry needs a much more efficient and scalable way of bringing power from the grid into the data center. Then we move into the AC to DC power supplies. These convert the AC power to 48 volts DC into the rack. A s the AI rack power continues to increase, these power supplies are reaching practical limits in terms of power density, efficiency, and thermal performance. The next challenge is power distribution. At relatively low voltages, such as 48 volts, delivering more power requires dramatically higher current. As rack power increases, the current flowing through the system quickly becomes enormous, driving significant I squared R losses, heat generation, and the need for impractically large amounts of copper. We see the same issue inside the compute tray. Power still has to be converted from the 48 volts down to 12 volts through an intermediate bus converter or an IBC. This additional DC to DC conversion stage introduces power losses, heat, and additional components that consume valuable board space. Finally, we reach what is becoming perhaps the most significant power delivery challenge, is the processor itself. Whether that's a GPU, a CPU, a TPU, or even a custom accelerator, these processors are consuming dramatically more power and demanding thousands of amps at sub -1 volt. When we talk about the AI infrastructure power wall, it isn't a single bottleneck. It's a cumulative effect of limitations across the entire power delivery chain. From the grid to the processor, legacy architectures are becoming pushed beyond what they are designed to support, creating compounding losses, thermal constraints, and power density limitations. As AI power continues to scale, simply improving each stage incrementally is no longer enough. We need to fundamentally rethink the power architecture from the grid all the way to the processor to break through these power walls and enable the next generation of AI compute. The question becomes, how do we redesign the architecture to break through each of these power walls? This slide shows how we see the architecture evolving and how Navitas is breaking through the AI infrastructure power wall. The fundamental change is the transition to 800 volt distribution, fewer conversion stages, and dramatically higher power density across the entire architecture. Starting at the grid, solid state transformers, or SSTs, can replace traditional line frequency transformers, providing a much compact, efficient, and intelligent approach to power conversion. These SSTs convert grid power directly to 800 volt DC while enabling bidirectional power flow, active power management, and seamless integration with battery energy storage systems, improving both power density and the resilience of the AI data center. Navitas' ultra-high voltage silicon carbide technology enables the high voltage, the high frequency power conversion required by these next generation SST architectures. At the next stage, increasing rack power is driving a transition towards much higher power density, requiring three-phase AC to DC power supplies. This allows significantly more power to be delivered to each rack while improving efficiency, power density, and thermal performance. Our high voltage silicon carbide MOSFETs support these higher power architectures with efficiency, switching performance, and reliability required for next generation power supplies. One of the biggest architectural changes is a move away from distributing 48 volts DC through the back plane to the individual IT trays. Instead, AC to DC and DC to DC power conversion moves into a power sidecar dedicated to supporting high power IT racks exceeding 1 MW, with 800 volt DC distributed directly to the rack and compute trays directly. This higher distribution voltage allows significantly more power to be delivered while keeping current at practical levels, reducing I squared R losses, heat, and the amount of copper required in the back plane. At this stage, high voltage silicon carbide and medium voltage gallium nitride become key technologies, delivering the efficiency, switching performance, and power density required for these next generation architectures. The next step is the 800 volt native compute tray. Now we can bring that higher voltage much closer to the processor and use very high density, high frequency DC to DC conversion close to where the power is actually consumed. Navitas recently collaborated with NVIDIA to demonstrate 800 volt to 6 volt and 800 volt to 12 volt DC to DC power delivery boards that eliminate the traditional 48 volt IBC stage. Now this improves system efficiency, reliability, cost, and compute density. Our high voltage and medium voltage gallium nitride FETs and GaN power ICs are ideally suited to this architecture, with a high frequency switching capability enabling smaller, more efficient, and significantly higher power density conversion directly within the compute tray. Finally, we get to the last few millimeters and perhaps the most critical stage of a power delivery. This is where vertical power delivery and embedded power delivery enabled by Claros' IVR technology fundamentally changes how we power the processor. In summary, the result is a fundamentally different grid-to-xPU architecture, higher voltage, fewer conversion stages, and higher power density. **Dan Kultran**  
*VP and General Manager of IVR Business Unit / Navitas Semiconductor*

Thanks, Llew. Let me double-click on that final delivery challenge and explain why it becomes so difficult at these current levels. Today, voltage regulators typically sit beside the processor, so the current has to travel across the board to reach the chip. As you just showed, we are asking that path to carry thousands of amps. That creates three challenges you see on this slide. First, electrical losses. At these currents, even a very small amount of resistance matters, and because resistive losses increase with the square of the current, doubling the current means four times the loss in the same path. As this slide highlights, lateral power delivery can lose up to 30% of the power that never reaches the processor. Second, these losses become heat. We are already trying to cool a very demanding hot processor, and now its power delivery system adds another thermal load. That makes cooling more complex and uses part of the system's thermal budget that could otherwise support more compute. Third, the board space. You can see in the picture how much space the voltage regulators occupy around the processor. As current demand grows, adding more power circuitry puts even more pressure on the limited space. Building on your point, this is why the final few inches matter so much. We need to shorten the current path and make power conversion more compact. That is the challenge Claros IVR technology stack is built to address. Let me show you how we bring that conversion closer to the processor. Let us talk a little bit about that next-generation power delivery architecture. The first step on the left-hand side is vertical power delivery, or VPD. This is already starting to happen as compute density increases. Instead of placing voltage regulators beside the processor, we move them directly underneath it on the backside of the board. Think of it as shortening the delivery path from centimeters to millimeters. A shorter path means less resistance, less energy loss as heat, and less parasitic inductance. That last point I made about inductance matters because AI workloads can change power demand very quickly. Lowering the inductance help the regulator respond faster to those changes and keep the processor running in a stable manner. The next step is embedded power delivery, or EPD. Here, we bring power conversion into the processor package or the substrate itself, further shortening the delivery path. We see EPD as the long-term destination for XPU power delivery. VPD gets us closer today, and EPD takes integration further as processor architectures evolve. Our IVR technology stack is designed to support both. Moving the regulator underneath the processor through VPD and EPD is an important step, but how we build that regulator matters just as much. Many early VPD implementations take the familiar components from lateral power delivery, the power transistor, discrete capacitors, and inductors, and assemble them into a module underneath the processor. That improves the delivery path, but the module can still be very bulky and the power still have to travel laterally. As power demand keeps increasing, simply rearranging those components only takes us so far. That is why we have spent years developing the Claros IVR technology stack. We designed the digital control, power transistors, high-density capacitors, high-power embeddable inductors, and packaging all together as one integrated solution. That lets us optimize how they work together and how much space they need. The controller coordinates power delivery and current sharing. The power devices perform the conversion. The integrated passive and advanced packaging help make the solution extremely compact and thin. This is the technology stack now we are unveiling as part of Navitas. Let me show you how it translates into products for both vertical and embedded power delivery architectures. On the left is Claros IVR technology stack, as we just discussed. In the middle are the two power delivery architectures, and on the right are the products that support them. The first is packaged IVR, or Navitas P-IVR. Think of it as an integrated IVR chip that can sit underneath the processor, replacing the larger modules I mentioned earlier. By integrating all the passive elements needed into our package, we can deliver more amps per given area. That true higher current density is especially valuable when space underneath the processor is limited. The second product is embedded IVR, or Navitas E-IVR. Here, we integrate silicon chiplets and advanced passive directly into the processor substrate. That brings conversion even closer and creates a path to support much higher current level. We develop both products because customers need improvements before embedded architectures are ready. Package IVR gives our customers a way to adopt our technology sooner, while embedded IVR supports deeper integration over time. Both of these are built on the same underlying Claros IVR technology stack. Having a great technology stack is not enough. Let us look at how we build a platform to scale. We start with a repeatable 10 amp building block. We can combine four of those building blocks into a 40 amp chip, or 16 into 160 amp chip. To illustrate compactness, our 40 amp chip, which fully integrates all passive elements, measures just 3.3 by 3.6 mm, delivering over 3.4 amp per millimeter square. Then we scale again by combining chips to meet the processor's total current requirement. The examples here show 64 chips delivering approximately 2,500 amps using the 40 amp configuration, or roughly 10,000 amps with the 160 amp configuration. T here are two levels of scalability, how many building blocks we put into a single chip, and how many chips we combine at the system level. This system level technology is called PowerArray. Our PowerArray technology uses an integrated digital controller, which coordinates the chips and manages how they share the load. That modular approach support both package and embedded power delivery architecture. We can build on the same foundation and configure it for different processors as their power requirement grows. Let us bring it all together. The Claros IVR technology gives us an integrated platform for delivering power closer to the processor with products for both vertical and embedded power delivery. For customers, the benefits are straightforward, more current and less space, faster response as workloads change, and an architecture that can scale with increasing power demands. Together with Navitas gallium nitride, high and ultra-high voltage silicon carbide technologies, this extends our power platform from the grid all the way to the XPU. Llew, you showed us how we can improve power delivery into the rack. What I have just covered is how we carry that improvement through the last few inches to the processor itself.
